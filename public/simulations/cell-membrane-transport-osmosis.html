Detailed Explanatory Notes: Cell Transport & Tonicity

1. Structure of the Cell Membrane

The cell membrane is composed of a phospholipid bilayer with hydrophilic (water-loving) heads facing outward and hydrophobic (water-fearing) tails facing inward. Embedded within this bilayer are transmembrane protein channels that allow specific ions and polar molecules to pass through.

2. Passive Transport (No Energy Required)

  • Simple Diffusion: Movement of small, nonpolar particles (and water molecules) directly through the lipid bilayer from an area of high concentration to low concentration.
  • Facilitated Diffusion: Transport of larger or charged molecules (such as glucose or ions) across the membrane using specific protein channels down their concentration gradient.
  • Osmosis: The net movement of water molecules across a semi-permeable membrane from a region of lower solute concentration (higher water potential) to a region of higher solute concentration.

3. Active Transport (Requires ATP Energy)

Active transport moves ions or molecules against their concentration gradient (from low concentration to high concentration). This process requires cellular energy in the form of ATP and relies on specialized protein pumps (e.g., the Sodium-Potassium Pump $\text{Na}^+/\text{K}^+$ ATPase).

4. Solutions & Cell Tonicity

  • Hypotonic Environment: Low solute concentration outside the cell. Water enters the cell by osmosis, causing animal cells to swell and potentially lyse (burst), while plant cells become turgid.
  • Isotonic Environment: Equal solute concentrations inside and outside the cell. Water moves in and out at equal rates, maintaining equilibrium.
  • Hypertonic Environment: High solute concentration outside the cell. Water moves out of the cell, causing the cell to shrink (crenation in red blood cells, plasmolysis in plant cells).
